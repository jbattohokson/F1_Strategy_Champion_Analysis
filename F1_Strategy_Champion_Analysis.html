Executive Summary

Raw car speed sets the range of results a team can reach. Strategy decides where in that range it finishes. I measured that gap across 68 races from 2022 to 2024 by ranking every full-time driver's race pace and comparing it with where they actually finished.

On the raw numbers, podium finishers gain 0.40 positions per race over their pace rank and Ferrari loses 0.12, which looks like half a position per race lost to strategy. That comparison doesn't hold. A car with the fastest race pace can't finish better than first, so front-runners score low by construction, and "podium" is defined by finishing well. Compared with other teams' cars at the same pace rank, Ferrari gains +0.33 positions per race (95% CI -0.05 to +0.67): average to slightly better.

Pit timing matches podium finishers (median first stop lap 18 vs. 17), and Ferrari's tire mix matches the rest of the field (49.7% of laps on HARD vs. 49.6%). Ferrari's cars average pace rank 4.1 against Red Bull's 3.2. The gap to the front is race pace, and strategy is already converting the pace Ferrari has.

Core Metric

The strategy delta: positions gained over raw pace

Strategy Delta = race-pace rank minus finish position. Pace rank comes from each driver's average lap time in that race. A positive delta means strategy moved a driver ahead of where pace alone would put him.

Why the raw delta misleads

A driver with the 7th-fastest average lap who finishes 5th scores +2. But the fastest car can only lose places, so pace rank 1 averages -0.52, and the podium group is selected for finishing well. The fair yardstick is the average delta other teams' cars get at the same pace rank, which is what the findings below use.

Analysis

What the data shows

01Ferrari converts its pace about as well as anyone outside the top two

Each team is compared with other teams' cars at the same race-pace rank, and the confidence interval resamples whole races. Ferrari gains +0.33 positions per race (95% CI -0.05 to +0.67). Only Red Bull (+0.54, CI +0.25 to +0.80) and Mercedes (+0.50, CI +0.12 to +0.85) are clearly above zero. Haas (-0.63) and Kick Sauber (-0.78) are the only teams clearly below.

What this means

The raw numbers made Ferrari look below average. Adjusted for pace, it's at or slightly above. The gap to Red Bull is mostly pace (4.1 vs. 3.2 average pace rank) plus about 0.2 positions of conversion that this data can't distinguish from noise.

02Pit timing already matches the front

Ferrari's median first stop is lap 18. Podium finishers stop on lap 17, the same as the rest of the top 10. Ferrari actually makes early-window stops (under a third of race distance) a little more often than podium finishers: 36.4% vs. 35.6%. The drivers who pit earliest are at the back of the grid (median lap 13), often reacting to problems.

What this rules out

Telling Ferrari to pit earlier has no support in the data. Its pit timing already matches the drivers it's trying to beat.

03Ferrari's tire mix matches the field

Version one compared Ferrari with podium finishers, a group that included Ferrari's own podiums. Against the other teams, Ferrari's compound mix is almost identical. Podium finishers run less HARD than everyone, Ferrari included.

Reading it

Running less HARD is probably a result of being at the front (clean air, track position, fewer forced stops), not a choice a midfield-pace car can copy. This data doesn't support changing Ferrari's compound strategy.

04Strategy matters most at a few circuits

Strategy delta varies most at the Austrian Grand Prix. Median degradation per lap on MEDIUM tires swung from 0.005 sec in 2022 to -0.020 in 2023 (lap times getting faster through a stint, likely as fuel burns off) and back to 0.005 in 2024, so circuit and season conditions change the right call.

Implication

Pre-race strategy prep should weight high-variance circuits more heavily, using that circuit's own history rather than a season average.

Model

Predicting finish position

M1Random Forest on 1,095 driver-races

Seven features predict finish position: gap to the leader, average lap time, degradation slope, first pit lap, stint count, soft tire share and pit count. Gap to the leader carries 73% of the importance. The original random 80/20 split scored R² = 0.73, but it put drivers from the same race in both training and test sets. Holding out whole races gives a more honest picture.

What it says

Pit timing, compound and degradation features add about 0.02 R² once race pace and grid position are known. Strategy moves the margins, and pace and qualifying decide most finishes.
